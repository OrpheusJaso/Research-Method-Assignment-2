\noindent
\textbf{Abstract}

\vspace{0.2cm}

\begin{justify}

Digital evidence is now central to prosecuting cybercrime, yet the field of digital forensics lacks standardised procedures and quantifiable measures of evidential reliability, An empirical analysis of 40 digital forensic reports found that most described their methods vaguely, none provided statistical support for reliability or validity, and most acknowledged limitations without quantifying error rates. This makes findings hard to reproduce and difficult to defend under legal admissibility standards such as the Daubert criteria. This proposal addresses that gap through four objectives: (1) a systematic review of current forensic methodologies and their documented deficiencies; (2) the design of a unified protocol covering identification, collection, preservation, examination, analysis and reporting, refined through expert review using the Delphi method; (3) the development of a reliability metric that scores digital evidence using factors such as the acquisition method, volatility, temporal decay, chain-of-custody integrity and tool validation status; (4) a controlled empirical evaluation comparing the standardised protocol against ad hoc baselines across simulated forensic scenarios. Performance will be measured using reliability scores, Cohen’s kappa, error rate, F1-score, chain-of-custody integrity rate and latency, with statistical significance testing. This study anticipates a validated protocol and metrics that improve reproducibility and give courts a transparent, objective basis for weighing digital evidence.

\vspace{0.3cm}

\end{justify}

\chapter{Introduction}

The present state of computer science allows for constant advancement in technology within the modern world. Of many innovations, AI has become a central subject within cybersecurity, for its rapid improvement of software through constant iteration and self-progression. 

However, this ability to rapidly improve is an emerging problem, as the sophisticated attacks; deepfakes, phishing, automation of malware, combined with this age of interconnected devices, the Internet of Things (IoT) give rise to more complications in updating the defenses against these attacks. 

As such, traditional methods for combating modern cybercrimes prove to be ineffective and outdated. According to \cite{sharma2024digital}, there is a large empirical gap within the research in digital forensics. Its empirical analysis of 40 digital forensic reports presents results with 82.5\% vague descriptions in their research methods, with 0% providing proper literature statistics for credibility and 90% acknowledging limitations in their paper. 

Furthermore, the lack of standardization in digital forensics creates difficulties in cases pertaining to cybercrime, as evidence can be insufficient, its procedure of collection unrecognized in legal systems, as well as make it difficult for validity in international cases. 
This highlights the importance of acknowledging that research in digital forensics is in its infancy, and in need of proper analysis.  

In light of these issues, this report aims to propose a standard protocol in gathering digital evidence and the establishment of reliability metrics to assist legal cases by removing uncertainty, introducing objectivity, and increasing the reliability of evidence. 



\chapter{Literature Review (Recap)}

From the literature that we’ve analysed during Assignment 1, there appears to be several broad consensus in regards to digital forensics and modern cybercrime:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The essentiality of digital forensics and the insufficiency of the traditional methods in combating cybercrime.} Research consistently indicates that digital forensics is vital for investigating and prosecuting cybercrimes through assisting law enforcement officers in locating, examining and protecting electronic data that can be used as digital evidence in court to identify suspects and establish legal facts. (\cite{arif2025unveiling}, \cite{ilyas2024role})

On the other hand, traditional forensic techniques are not adequate to combat contemporary cybercrime. The sophistication of cybercrime methodology utilised by perpetrators such as encryption and anti-forensic softwares that are able to conceal their activities from relevant investigators while allowing malicious actors to evade the standard security filters; large data volumes which, when analysed through traditional means, is slow and at times impossible due to the size of the data volume are clear signals that the traditional methods are insufficient and at times detrimental against combating cybercrime, as events like security breaches can cause massive damage before investigators are able to identify them through the traditional methods.

\item \textbf{Lack of standardisation in digital forensic methodologies.} Research articles obtained highlight a clear lack of standardised protocols across the field of digital forensics. Different investigators frequently apply divergent methodologies, which often leads to inconsistent analytical outcomes in resultant research articles and reports. (\cite{ilyas2024role}, \cite{arif2025unveiling}, \cite{sharma2024digital})

\item \textbf{Possible upgrades towards digital forensics through AI and Zero Trust Architecture.} As cybercriminals have utilised AI software for generating steganographic code to mask hidden information while they commit cybercrimes (\cite{osamor2026comprehensive}, \cite{sanna2025improving}), various articles have suggested the implementation of artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning and deep learning within digital forensics as aid against said AI-based attacks. Through proper utilisation of AI as an assistant in digital forensics, systems are able to analyse large datasets in a short time, automate dull and repetitive duties and identify possible configurations that human investigators may overlook. This allows professionals to operate with much greater speed and precision in comparison.

In terms of Zero Trust, a couple of research articles proposed utilising a Zero Trust Architecture, which operates on a “never trust, always verify” mantra (\cite{prajwalasimha2025ai}, \cite{arif2025unveiling}). This allows for a much more effective protection mechanism against complex cyber threats and deceptive email campaigns. Besides that, they also suggest implementing a layered defense strategy as well, to better filter out cyberthreats alongside providing safe and secure protection against critical assets.

\end{itemize}

\chapter{Problem Statement}

\section{Context}
Present-day Investigators specializing in digital forensics have a critical role in providing assistance to law enforcement in regards to digital crimes, particularly ones involving computers. These specialists influence how electronic data is collected, examined and stored for legal cases, determining how attorneys may approach or strategize their court proceedings.

As crimes on the internet become increasingly complex, such as advanced persistent threats (APTs), ransomware, deepfakes and methods that disrupt or even prevent investigations, legal systems will consequently depend heavily on digital data as evidence.

In many nations, judges follow a standard such as the Daubert criteria (\cite{carranza2021software}). As such, it is expected that the courts require specialists to show their method of gathering data has specified measured mistakes, have undergone checks by other scientists, and ultimately approved by the scientific community.

\section{Gap}
The field of digital forensics lacks uniform procedures for its operations. According to \cite{sharma2024digital}, where a study of 40 digital forensic reports was made, researchers found multiple specific weaknesses. The findings reveal that 33 of those documents used unclear language to describe how investigators performed their tasks. There is no instance where the authors included mathematical data to prove consistency or accuracy in their results. 

Despite 36 reports addressing flaws through noting constraints existing in their studies, the specific quantitative data measuring the frequency of mistakes were not included in their articles. 

As previously indicated, the lack of fixed rules causes different specialists to reach different conclusions when they examine identical datasets. As a result, the process of repeating investigations to verify results proves difficult for investigators to achieve. 

Considering these factors, legal systems may refuse to accept digital evidence as viable proof. Paradoxically however, there does not exist a system to measure the trust a user can place in digital data. This information is crucial in providing the framework for specialists to gather data, such as the storage logs remaining intact and the details on when events occurred. 

Additionally, the legal system is currently without concrete rules to determine how much importance a judge should give to this evidence.

\section{Solution}
We propose a process where investigators use a uniform digital forensic protocol and a system that measures reliability through quantitative data to evaluate digital evidence. The protocol describes the required steps during every forensic stage, including how investigators identify, collect, preserve, examine, analyze and report data. Moreover, we incorporated a reliability metric that assigns a numerical score between one and ten based on factors that researchers have validated through observation.

The factors as mentioned include the method used to acquire data, the rate at which data changes, the time taken before recovery as well as the degree of completion in the chain of custody. 

By creating a framework that experts can successfully repeat and explain clearly, it is anticipated that uncertainty will be removed, resulting in legal systems being able to admit digital evidence based on facts. Ultimately, this paper aims to create a method for electronic files to be consistent and neutral to ease the process of legal evaluations on computer crimes.

\section{Objective 3 (Addresses RQ3)}

To formulate and validate a reliability metric system for digital evidence that defines the factors of making digital evidence reliable and assigns quantifiable scores (1–10 scale) based on empirically derived weightings for the acquisition method, evidence volatility, temporal delay, chain of custody integrity, and tool validation status, tested against a corpus of at least 100 historical forensic cases to establish scoring accuracy and inter-rater reliability (Cohen’s $\kappa \geq 0.80$) and to assess its usability within the court system within twelve months of project commencement.

\textbf{SMART Criteria}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Specific}: Develop a numerical scoring system for digital evidence reliability and define the underlying reliability constructs for court evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Measurable}: Validate against 100 cases; achieve Cohen’s $\kappa \geq 0.80$ for inter-rater reliability; document scoring accuracy compared to expert consensus; assess court usability.
    \item \textbf{Achievable}: Historical case data available through law enforcement partnerships and public court records.
    \item \textbf{Relevant}: Provides courts with objective criteria for assessing evidentiary weight and reliability.

\section{Objective 4 (Addresses RQ4)}

To empirically evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed standardised protocol and reliability metric through a controlled experimental study comparing forensic outcomes (reliability scores, reproducibility via Cohen’s Kappa, error rates, and processing latency) between the standardised protocol and baseline ad hoc methodologies across at least 20 simulated forensic scenarios, and through a mock court setting assessing the repeatability of forensic analysis and the admissibility of digital evidence, with results demonstrating statistically significant improvement (p < 0.05) in reproducibility and error reduction within eighteen months of project commencement.

\textbf{SMART Criteria}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Specific}: Conduct controlled experiments comparing standardised vs. ad hoc protocols, plus a mock court assessment of repeatability and admissibility.
    \item \textbf{Measurable}: 20+ scenarios; statistical significance at p < 0.05; improvement in Cohen’s $\kappa$ and error rate reduction documented; mock court admissibility ratings collected.
    \item \textbf{Achievable}: Utilises established forensic datasets, controlled laboratory conditions, and mock court participants such as legal practitioners or mock judges.

\chapter{Research Methodology}

\section{Research Design}

In terms of Research Design, we’ve elected to use an empirical evaluation design through the methodology of controlled and repeated measures. The methodology will require forensic datasets, which will be used to be processed through the proposed standardised protocol alongside the baseline ad hoc protocols, while using constants to ensure that the protocol is the only systematically varied factor besides from the type of evidence obtained from the dataset. Afterwards, each condition obtained from the processed protocols will be run through independent trials from varying pipeline instances to support significance testing alongside providing multiple evidence samples per category to avoid cramming all the conclusions into a single case.

The dependent variables for this methodology would be the reliability score for each protocol, the reproducibility of the results amongst analysts (through Cohen’s $\kappa$), integrity of the evidence from the chain of custody, time taken for protocols to produce a report and the error rate in comparison to the ground truth.

The main reasoning behind using empirical evaluation is that in accordance to the reliability queries under the Daubert standard, a legal report reliability claim is required to have a known, repeatable and quantifiable error rate alongside the procedure being tested, peer-reviewed, published and generally accepted by the relevant community, in this situation being the Digital Forensics field (\cite{carranza2021software}). Through the use of a controlled empirical design and by the nature of the protocol being the sole variable in said environment, we are able to obtain a known error rate with statistical confidence. In terms of using other designs, the ground truth cannot be obtained from methods such as analysing the data obtained from live caseworks as there is no independently verifiable correct answer from said cases, hence an error rate is unable to be measured using said design and it can be deemed that empirical evaluation is the best suited for this specific research.

% 9. CONCLUSION
% =========================================

This proposal aims to respond to a documented gap: digital forensics lacks standardised procedures and quantifiable reliability measures, which weakens the reproducibility and legal defensibility of digital evidence. We propose a unified forensic protocol, a reliability metric for scoring digital evidence, and a controlled empirical evaluation that tests both against ad hoc baselines using measurable criteria such as Cohen's kappa, error rate, F1-score and chain-of-custody integrity. Together these aim to replace subjective, inconsistent practice with a repeatable and testable approach.

As cybercrime grows more complex, courts will inevitably have to rely more heavily upon digital evidence, which must be trustworthy and open to scrutiny. Grounding digital forensic practices through standardised protocols alongside metrics for the reliability of evidence within legal reports produced from said practices offers a path to making digital evidence more consistent, transparent and defensible in a court of law.
