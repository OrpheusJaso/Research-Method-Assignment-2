\textbf{Abstract}

\vspace{0.2cm}

\begin{justify}

Digital evidence is now central to prosecuting cybercrime, yet the field of digital forensics lacks standardised procedures and quantifiable measures of evidential reliability, An empirical analysis of 40 digital forensic reports found that most described their methods vaguely, none provided statistical support for reliability or validity, and most acknowledged limitations without quantifying error rates. This makes findings hard to reproduce and difficult to defend under legal admissibility standards such as the Daubert criteria. This proposal addresses that gap through four objectives: (1) a systematic review of current forensic methodologies and their documented deficiencies; (2) the design of a unified protocol covering identification, collection, preservation, examination, analysis and reporting, refined through expert review using the Delphi method; (3) the development of a reliability metric that scores digital evidence using factors such as the acquisition method, volatility, temporal decay, chain-of-custody integrity and tool validation status; (4) a controlled empirical evaluation comparing the standardised protocol against ad hoc baselines across simulated forensic scenarios. Performance will be measured using reliability scores, Cohen’s kappa, error rate, F1-score, chain-of-custody integrity rate and latency, with statistical significance testing. This study anticipates a validated protocol and metrics that improve reproducibility and give courts a transparent, objective basis for weighing digital evidence.

\vspace{0.3cm}

\end{justify}

\chapter{Introduction}

The present state of computer science allows for constant advancement in technology within the modern world. Of many innovations, AI has become a central subject within cybersecurity, for its rapid improvement of software through constant iteration and self-progression. 

However, this ability to rapidly improve is an emerging problem, as the sophisticated attacks; deepfakes, phishing, automation of malware, combined with this age of interconnected devices, the Internet of Things (IoT) give rise to more complications in updating the defenses against these attacks. 

As such, traditional methods for combating modern cybercrimes prove to be ineffective and outdated. According to \cite{sharma2024digital}, there is a large empirical gap within the research in digital forensics. Its empirical analysis of 40 digital forensic reports presents results with 82.5\% vague descriptions in their research methods, with 0\% providing proper literature statistics for credibility and 90\% acknowledging limitations in their paper. 

Furthermore, the lack of standardization in digital forensics creates difficulties in cases pertaining to cybercrime, as evidence can be insufficient, its procedure of collection unrecognized in legal systems, as well as make it difficult for validity in international cases. 
This highlights the importance of acknowledging that research in digital forensics is in its infancy, and in need of proper analysis.  

In light of these issues, this report aims to propose a standard protocol in gathering digital evidence and the establishment of reliability metrics to assist legal cases by removing uncertainty, introducing objectivity, and increasing the reliability of evidence. 



\chapter{Literature Review (Recap)}

From the literature that we’ve analysed during Assignment 1, there appears to be several broad consensus in regards to digital forensics and modern cybercrime:

\begin{itemize}

Besides that, they also suggest implementing a layered defense strategy as well, to better filter out cyberthreats alongside providing safe and secure protection against critical assets.

\chapter{Problem Statement}

\section{Context}
Present-day Investigators specializing in digital forensics have a critical role in providing assistance to law enforcement in regards to digital crimes, particularly ones involving computers. These specialists influence how electronic data is collected, examined and stored for legal cases, determining how attorneys may approach or strategize their court proceedings.

As crimes on the internet become increasingly complex, such as advanced persistent threats (APTs), ransomware, deepfakes and methods that disrupt or even prevent investigations, legal systems will consequently depend heavily on digital data as evidence.

In many nations, judges follow a standard such as the Daubert criteria (\cite{carranza2021software}). As such, it is expected that the courts require specialists to show their method of gathering data has specified measured mistakes, have undergone checks by other scientists, and ultimately approved by the scientific community.

\section{Gap}
The field of digital forensics lacks uniform procedures for its operations. According to \cite{sharma2024digital}, where a study of 40 digital forensic reports was made, researchers found multiple specific weaknesses. The findings reveal that 33 of those documents used unclear language to describe how investigators performed their tasks. There is no instance where the authors included mathematical data to prove consistency or accuracy in their results. 

Despite 36 reports addressing flaws through noting constraints existing in their studies, the specific quantitative data measuring the frequency of mistakes were not included in their articles. 

As previously indicated, the lack of fixed rules causes different specialists to reach different conclusions when they examine identical datasets. As a result, the process of repeating investigations to verify results proves difficult for investigators to achieve. 

Considering these factors, legal systems may refuse to accept digital evidence as viable proof. Paradoxically however, there does not exist a system to measure the trust a user can place in digital data. This information is crucial in providing the framework for specialists to gather data, such as the storage logs remaining intact and the details on when events occurred. 

Additionally, the legal system is currently without concrete rules to determine how much importance a judge should give to this evidence.

\section{Solution}
We propose a process where investigators use a uniform digital forensic protocol and a system that measures reliability through quantitative data to evaluate digital evidence. The protocol describes the required steps during every forensic stage, including how investigators identify, collect, preserve, examine, analyze and report data. Moreover, we incorporated a reliability metric that assigns a numerical score between one and ten based on factors that researchers have validated through observation.

The factors as mentioned include the method used to acquire data, the rate at which data changes, the time taken before recovery as well as the degree of completion in the chain of custody. 

By creating a framework that experts can successfully repeat and explain clearly, it is anticipated that uncertainty will be removed, resulting in legal systems being able to admit digital evidence based on facts. Ultimately, this paper aims to create a method for electronic files to be consistent and neutral to ease the process of legal evaluations on computer crimes.

\chapter{Research Methodology}

\section{Research Design}

In terms of Research Design, we’ve elected to use an empirical evaluation design through the methodology of controlled and repeated measures. The methodology will require forensic datasets, which will be used to be processed through the proposed standardised protocol alongside the baseline ad hoc protocols, while using constants to ensure that the protocol is the only systematically varied factor besides from the type of evidence obtained from the dataset. Afterwards, each condition obtained from the processed protocols will be run through independent trials from varying pipeline instances to support significance testing alongside providing multiple evidence samples per category to avoid cramming all the conclusions into a single case.

The dependent variables for this methodology would be the reliability score for each protocol, the reproducibility of the results amongst analysts (through Cohen’s $\kappa$), integrity of the evidence from the chain of custody, time taken for protocols to produce a report and the error rate in comparison to the ground truth.

The main reasoning behind using empirical evaluation is that in accordance to the reliability queries under the Daubert standard, a legal report reliability claim is required to have a known, repeatable and quantifiable error rate alongside the procedure being tested, peer-reviewed, published and generally accepted by the relevant community, in this situation being the Digital Forensics field (\cite{carranza2021software}). Through the use of a controlled empirical design and by the nature of the protocol being the sole variable in said environment, we are able to obtain a known error rate with statistical confidence. In terms of using other designs, the ground truth cannot be obtained from methods such as analysing the data obtained from live caseworks as there is no independently verifiable correct answer from said cases, hence an error rate is unable to be measured using said design and it can be deemed that empirical evaluation is the best suited for this specific research.

\subsection{General Principles}
This section outlines the general principles that must be followed when the investigation is carried out:
\\
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The evidence must immediately be seized upon discovery.
    \item The original evidence must not be tampered or directly examined where it may alter its initial state.
    \item The evidence to be examined by forensic tools must only be examined by its copy, and never the original source. This prevents data overwriting in volatile memory.
    \item If access to original data cannot be avoided, every action must be recorded in its investigation. 
    \item The investigation must be replicable by a third party.
    \item All actions made in the investigation must be recorded through a reliable logging system. 
    \item Throughout the investigation, an audit trail must be maintained on all actions done on the evidence, whether original or copied. The details related to the trail must be recorded extensively, such as the investigator carrying out the action, the date, time, etc. 
\end{enumerate}

\\
\\

\subsubsection{Protocol 3: Verification \& Preparation}

\begin{itemize}

\subsubsection{Protocol 4: Examination \& Analysis}

The examination stage of the investigation must be done carefully to ensure the data is thoroughly assessed for analysis. 

The examination of data requires a development of a suitable plan. The following lists out the factors to consider when executing data examination. 

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Data filtration}: Forensic tools must be identified to filter data and extract only relevant data through a predefined criteria. The criteria to be established may include file type, user details, timestamps, search keywords, application, or other characteristics which ensures reproducibility. 
    \item \textbf{System bypassing \& data-access barriers}: Data/code may be hidden behind features provided by certain software, such as the system’s OS and applications or the data may be behind a password or encrypted. Thus, investigators must create a strategy to overcome such systems using known techniques. 
    \item \textbf{Forensic tool \& techniques selection}: The forensic tool selected by investigators must be appropriate to the context of the evidence, and the examination objectives.
\end{enumerate}

The following is the procedure required to develop a data examination plan. 

\begin{itemize}

    \item \checkbox\ Establish data filter criteria

    \item \checkbox\ Identify potential data-access barriers
    \item \checkbox\ Strategize overcoming data-access barriers
    \item \checkbox\ Select suitable forensic tools/techniques 
    \item \checkbox\ Record all forensic tool details (names, versions and configurations)
    \item \checkbox\ Record procedures for executing Examination
    \item \checkbox\ Record all details pertaining to the examination 
\end{itemize}

Once the examination plan has been defined, the investigator must execute the plan through a systematic, controlled approach, with appropriate documentation. There are several factors to consider when carrying out the analysis. 

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Data access \& recovery}: When accessing data using the selected forensic tool, investigators must identify the data that are inaccessible, with proper documentation of its recovery and the techniques used.
    \item \textbf{Data filtering}: Data is filtered through an established criteria made by the investigation to ensure resources are not spent on irrelevant data. This process must be done carefully, as to not exclude evidence that may be crucial. All data that are excluded must be documented accordingly. 
    \item \textbf{Individual data analysis}: Individual data properties are examined to determine its content, timestamps, metadata, any other relevant information to the investigation. 
    \item \textbf{Data relationship analysis}: Investigators must identify relationships between information, which may support conclusions made. 
    \item \textbf{Comparison and contradictions}: Findings made from different techniques are compared and searched for any contradictions, which may warrant re-evaluation. 
    \item \textbf{Critical Analysis}: Investigators must critically evaluate the obtained findings and relate evidence with one another. Resulting in creating conclusions with supporting evidence. Additionally, investigators must ensure that their reasoning behind conclusions considers alternate possibilities. As such, all interpretations must be recorded, and having a clear distinction between fact from observation, with interpretation.
    \item \textbf{Documentation of uncertainty}: Any limitations by the forensic tools, or any data inaccessibility, or uncertainties in any findings have to be documented extensively. This is to ensure any information is not excluded when building a defensible forensic case. 
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Protocol 5: Reporting}

The synthesizing of a concise, comprehensive report of the investigation’s findings is crucial for presenting evidence of the case, as well as giving a detailed log of every action in the case to prevent misinterpretation. 
\\
The following outlines the sections required to be included into the formal documentation.


\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Case information}: All records, such as the IDs, details and names of associated people, device and evidence regarding the case must be included.
    \item \textbf{Scope}: The investigators must outline the specific scope and objections of the investigation.
    \item \textbf{Forensic methodology}: The details that must be included are as follows: 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Details of forensic tools used (ie: tool name, tool version)
        \item Settings/configurations of device
        \item State of device
        \item Part of evidence examined
        \item Any related procedures in the data examination 
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Evidence integrity}: the documentation must include the hash values calculated, the record of copies made in the investigation, the information regarding the chain-of-custody, and the results of the evidence integrity verification. 
    \item \textbf{Synthesized findings}: The results observed, supporting artefacts, interpretations and analyses must be recorded.
    \item \textbf{Limitations}: Document the unrecoverable data of the case, missing evidence, tool limitations, uncertainty in interpretation, and any details that may limit an aspect of the case. 
\end{enumerate}

When corroborating information and creating the report, it is imperative to ensure that there is a clear separation between the observations/facts of the case and the interpretations made by the investigator.

\subsection{The Delphi Method}
After obtaining the evaluated data from the standard protocol and the ad hoc procedure; the results are further analysed by the established approach, the Delphi method, known for its accuracy in judging a solution to a problem and its successful results, according to the (insert source). The aim is to gather unbiased expert opinion on the results to determine which of the two procedures are preferred. Generally, the method is a qualitative approach with many iterations to form a final consensus, mainly by converting subjective opinions into objective results. 

There are many variations to the delphi method, and we have chosen a modified approach that we have collectively agreed would be most reliable. The main data collection is by structured questionnaires through online means, for flexibility. The experts must have deep knowledge on the subject and years of experience in digital forensics, either as a researcher or an investigator. The content of the questionnaire contains the analysis of the data as well as raw statistics for the expert’s own interpretation; each round of MCQ will have a corresponding anonymous discussion between the experts. In total, three rounds of questioning will determine the preferred choice, with all results and opinions extensively documented. 

\chapter{Expected Outcomes}

The study anticipates the following outcomes, tied to your four objectives:

\section{Conclusion}

\textbf{Summary of contributions}: This proposal aims to respond to a documented gap: digital forensics lacks standardised procedures and quantifiable reliability measures, which weakens the reproducibility and legal defensibility of digital evidence. We propose a unified forensic protocol, a reliability metric for scoring digital evidence, and a controlled empirical evaluation that tests both against ad hoc baselines using measurable criteria such as Cohen's kappa, error rate, F1-score and chain-of-custody integrity. Together these aim to replace subjective, inconsistent practice with a repeatable and testable approach.

\textbf{Final remarks}: As cybercrime grows more complex, courts will inevitably have to rely more heavily upon digital evidence, which must be trustworthy and open to scrutiny. Grounding digital forensic practices through standardised protocols alongside metrics for the reliability of evidence within legal reports produced from said practices offers a path to making digital evidence more consistent, transparent and defensible in a court of law.
